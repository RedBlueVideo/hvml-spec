# Episodic Identity in Media Registries — Research Briefing

**Status:** Research memo, compiled 2026-08-05 by a background research agent; primary documentation fetched and read for every claim except the two marked as practice-based assessment. Uncommitted pending author review. Feeds the open design discussion: `about` on `series`/`group`, and §2.3 amendments for episodic works in `docs/2026-08-03-identity-versions-presentations.md`.
**Scope:** how EIDR, ISAN, and Wikidata (plus MusicBrainz, ISSN, and Crossref as adjacent precedent) assign identity to series, seasons, and episodes; synthesis mapped to HVML's year-namespaced IRI scheme at the end, labeled as analysis.

Sourcing notes: EIDR's PDFs have vanished from eidr.org's current site (the old `/documents/` paths 404), so the two EIDR documents were retrieved via the Wayback Machine at their canonical URLs; the ISAN support article likewise (its live page sits behind a Cloudflare challenge). Everything quoted below was read from the documents themselves, not from secondary summaries.

## 1. EIDR

Sources: *Best Practices and Use Cases for Episodic Records* v34, 2020-07-13 (https://www.eidr.org/documents/Best%20Practices%20and%20Use%20Cases%20for%20Episodic%20Records.pdf) and *EIDR 2.6 Data Fields Reference* v2.2a, 2020-07-10 (https://www.eidr.org/documents/EIDR%202.6%20Data%20Fields%20Reference.pdf).

**The hierarchy.** EIDR's registration tree has four record types: Collection (Series, Season, Compilation), Abstraction (movies, episodes, one-time-only TV shows), Edit, and Manifestation. A Series is "an abstract collection of Seasons and Episodes"; Seasons are optional ("Series need not have Seasons... may be used as convenient grouping records"); Episodes are Abstractions that may be children of either a Season or the Series directly (season-less Specials and Pilots are the common case). Edits and Manifestations hang below Episodes only — the Edits best-practices doc confirms Series and Seasons are grouping identifiers with no Edits of their own.

**ID assignment.** Every record at every level — Series, Season, Episode, Edit, Manifestation, Compilation — receives a full EIDR Content ID: a DOI under prefix `10.5240` with a 5×4-hex-digit suffix plus check character ("The Content prefix in the Registry is `10.5240`", Data Fields Reference §1.4). So yes, a season is a first-class DOI, indistinguishable in form from a movie's ID: the sample Season record's parent reference is `10.5240/0C73-26CE-1E02-8827-F63C-H`. The doc notes "all EIDR IDs look alike" — nothing in the ID encodes level, year, or title.

**Parent references.** Child-to-parent, required, by DOI: `ExtraObjectMetadata/SeasonInfo/Parent` holds the Series' Content ID; `EpisodeInfo/Parent` holds the Series' or Season's. Ordering is metadata, not identity: `SeasonInfo/SequenceNumber` for seasons; `DistributionNumber` (plus `HouseSequence` and `AlternateNumber`) for episodes. Children inherit base fields (mode, language, credits, orgs) from the parent unless overridden.

**Series dates.** `ReleaseDate` is required on a Series and is defined as "The date or year of the first released Episode, even if it has not yet been registered in EIDR" (outlying pilots and specials excluded). The general definition: "The date the work was first released, broadcast, offered for retail sale, or otherwise made available for public viewing. Either a full date (preferred) or a four-digit year." The end of the run is a separate, optional field: `SeriesInfo/EndDate`, "the date or year of the final released Episode," absent while the series is ongoing, and constrained to be ≥ ReleaseDate. So EIDR dates a series by its premiere, as a single frozen value, and treats the range as premiere + optional end — not as a mutable span. Season ReleaseDates must fall within the series' range.

**Disambiguating same-titled series.** Three mechanisms:

- Every registration is preceded by a de-duplication match over the metadata (title, date, participants); the EndDate guidance says to include it "whenever known, as this is valuable for data validation of child records, particularly when there is more than one series with the same name" — i.e., dates are the working disambiguator.
- Mid-run renames do not split the record: "Bizarre Foods with Andrew Zimmern became Bizarre Foods America while Valerie became The Hogan Family. This does not constitute a new Series, just a new name," recorded as `AlternateResourceName`. Survivor's individually-named seasons stay under one Series record.
- Remakes and reboots split: "When a Series is remade or retooled after a significant production gap it is generally considered to be a new Series." Verified concretely for Doctor Who: EIDR carries two separate Series DOIs — classic `10.5240/FE4D-3898-7072-0277-730A-Z`, 2005 revival `10.5240/11A2-C069-8DC3-9801-52A9-9` (observed via Wikidata's `P2704` claims on Q39243428/Q39243484; https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Q39243484).

**Season identity in practice.** Season names are deliberately suppressed: "Do not use Season Names that can be derived from other metadata... let the Registry generate the name automatically"; only genuinely unique names (Survivor: Redemption Island) are user-supplied. And for distribution, EIDR routes around Season records entirely: bundles of Edits are identified with Compilation records ("In many distribution models, the items being distributed are identified with EIDR Edit IDs rather than Episode Abstraction IDs... it is convenient to have an identifier for the collection of Edits. This is done using EIDR Compilation objects."). A footnote adds a useful survey datum: "IMDb does not provide Season IDs (only Series and Episode)."

## 2. ISAN / V-ISAN

Sources: *ISAN User Guide* v2.4, 2016-09-05 (https://www.isan.org/docs/isan_user_guide.pdf) and the ISAN support article "ISAN creation: episodic works," updated 2022-04-12 (https://support.isan.org/hc/en-us/articles/360018128591-ISAN-creation-episodic-works, retrieved via Wayback).

**Number structure.** 24 hex digits plus two check characters: a 12-digit root segment, a 4-digit episode/part segment, an 8-digit version segment. A stand-alone work (feature film) takes episode segment `0000`; an episode of a serial takes a nonzero episode segment; version `0000-0000` means no version. V-ISAN is the same identifier read with a populated version segment: "the unique identifier composed of a registered ISAN in combination with an appended version segment." All versions of a work share the first 16 digits; version hierarchies (versions of versions) live in registry metadata, created automatically at allocation.

**Series and seasons (per the 2016 guide).** The guide is categorical: "Each episode within series or other episodic audiovisual work must have its own 'episodic' ISAN. Series are identified in the ISAN system with the root ISAN, i.e. the first 12 digits of an ISAN, shared between all ISAN allocated to the episodes of that serial. Groups of episodes including seasons are not identified with an ISAN, instead the group of episode information (i.e. the season number) is defined for each episode in the 'Season number' field within the episode metadata. No single ISAN can be assigned to cover a serial or a season of a serial as a whole." (§3.4). The series is thus a derived prefix, not an issued identifier — though resolving just the 12-digit root against the registry returns serial-level metadata (the guide demonstrates with Orange Is the New Black, root `0000-0003-BCB4`). Episode-segment values carry no order semantics: "No apparent sequential allocations in the episode segment shall imply the order in which episodes were produced or transmitted."

**The schema has since evolved.** The 2022 support article describes the current registry: "Previously, Episodes were regrouped under a Serial Header representing the Series... The Serial Header of the previous schema is replaced by a Series Record described with a full set of metadata (similar to episodes)," plus optional **Group** records between Series and Episodes — "A typical grouping of Episodes are Seasons, but other kind of groups are possible such as sets of alternate episode edits... or groups of episode compilations." An episode can belong to several groups. Two registration-flow details matter: the series' root ISAN "is only delivered after registration of the series and registration of the first Group or Episode" (no root is reserved on the series form alone); and the Group registration steps end with "Get the new ISAN" — so seasons, as Groups, now do receive ISANs, superseding the 2016 guide's "no season ISAN" rule. NOTE: how a Group's ISAN is encoded (presumably an allocation from the same root + episode-segment space) is not stated in any public document reachable; treat that encoding as unverified.

**Ongoing series.** Nothing structural: the root persists indefinitely and each new episode takes a fresh episode segment at registration time. No end date is required or encoded anywhere in the identifier. (Capacity arithmetic, ours not theirs: 4 hex digits cap a root at 65,535 episode-segment allocations.) Works not yet complete can take an In-Development ISAN — an identifier reservation whose metadata remains private until activated as a normal ISAN (§4.4). Descriptive metadata for each additional episode is inherited from the most recently registered episode to ease bulk registration (§3.4).

## 3. Wikidata (and Wikipedia naming)

Sources: property pages https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Property:P179 and https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Property:P4908; live SPARQL/API queries run 2026-08-05 against query.wikidata.org and wikidata.org; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Naming_conventions_(television).

**Structure.** Three classes: `television series` (Q5398426), `television series season` (Q3464665), `television series episode` (Q21191270). A season links to its series with `P179` ("part of the series", a subproperty of `P361` "part of") qualified by `P1545` (series ordinal). An episode links to its series with `P179` and to its season with `P4908` ("season"), the latter qualified by `P1545` and `P179`; `P1545` is constrained to qualifier use only. `P155`/`P156` (follows / followed by) order siblings, typically as qualifiers.

**Coverage.** Not every season of every notable show has an item — not close. Counts as of 2026-08-05: 87,935 series items, 26,378 season items, 181,196 episode items. Major shows are fully itemized (every Doctor Who season and revival series, 40+ items, each with `P179` → Q34316 and, where entered, its own `P580`); the long tail of series has episode items without season items, or neither. Season items exist where Wikipedia season articles or episode-list articles exist to anchor them.

**Dating.** Series and season items are dated with `P580` (start time = first air date) and `P582` (end time), not `P571` (inception). Verified: Q34316 Doctor Who has `P580` = 1963-11-23 and no `P582` (ongoing — an open range is simply a missing end); the 2005 item has `P580` = 2005-03-26; season items carry their own `P580` (Doctor Who season 1: 1963-11-23). Episodes conventionally take `P577` (publication date); asserted from general practice, not verified against a specific episode item.

**Revivals — the instructive case.** English Wikipedia treats Doctor Who as one article, and Wikidata mirrors that with one primary item, Q34316 (1963–present, multiple era-qualified `P2437` season counts and `P1113` episode counts). But the external registries all split at the 2005 premiere — IMDb `tt0056751` vs `tt0436992`, TMDb `121` vs `57243`, TheTVDB, EIDR's two DOIs — so Wikidata grew bridge items: Q39243428 "Doctor Who (1963–1989)" and Q39243484 "Doctor Who (2005)," which hold the era-specific external IDs, while Q34316's own IMDb claim is qualified with `P8327` ("applies to part") pointing at the classic-era item. Where Wikipedia unifies and registries split, Wikidata pays for it with extra items to keep equivalence links one-to-one. Where Wikipedia itself splits — Battlestar Galactica (Q2367132, 1970s vs Q237072, 2004–2009), The Office (Q23829 British, Q23831 American, Q130441989 Australian) — the items split cleanly and no bridging is needed. The Doctor Who season items also show ordinal-namespace restart inside one series: classic "season 1–26," revival "series 1–15."

**Wikipedia article naming** (WP:NCTV): base title if unambiguous; "(TV series)" when disambiguation is required; the country adjective is "the preferred disambiguation method when needed to distinguish shows with the same title from different countries" ("The Office (American TV series)"); the debut year is used "when there are shows with the same title within the same country" ("Battlestar Galactica (1978 TV series)" vs "(2004 TV series)"). Season articles follow the parent's scheme. Two implications for human-readable naming: the year chosen is the premiere year, frozen — article titles are not revised as a show runs; and year is the second-resort disambiguator, after country, which a year-only path scheme cannot express.

## 4. Adjacent precedent

**MusicBrainz.** A release group "embraces the overall concept of an album" while a release is "something you can buy" (https://musicbrainz.org/doc/Release_Group). The release group carries a `first-release-date` derived from its earliest contained release — verified via the API: Blind Guardian's *Nightfall in Middle-Earth* release group reports `first-release-date: 1998-04-24` while containing remasters through 2007+ (https://musicbrainz.org/ws/2/release-group/3a5d9bb8-8e19-379d-b294-ee8fcf743f77). Precedent: the work-level record is dated by earliest release and never re-dated by reissues; the IDs themselves are opaque UUIDs.

**ISSN.** The ISSN identifies a serial at the title-in-a-medium level and is "a digital code without any intrinsic meaning" — no year, no country, no content information (https://www.issn.org/understanding-the-issn/what-is-an-issn/). Continuing resources have "no predetermined end" and keep one ISSN for the entire run; an ongoing serial needs no completion date because the identifier asserts nothing temporal. A significant title change forces a new ISSN. Print and online editions take separate ISSNs, grouped by a linking ISSN-L (https://www.issn.org/understanding-the-issn/the-issn-l/).

**Crossref/DOI.** Articles get DOIs; the journal — the ongoing serial — is identified by its ISSN. Journal title-level DOIs exist and Crossref encourages them, but they are optional, and mandatory only as a stand-in when no ISSN has been assigned yet; volume- and issue-level DOIs are possible and fee-free but likewise optional; "any changes requiring a new ISSN should result in a new title-level DOI" (https://support.crossref.org/hc/en-us/articles/214569903-Journal-level-DOIs; https://www.crossref.org/documentation/principles-practices/journals/). In citation practice, the article DOI plus journal name/ISSN does the work; issue-level DOIs are rarely minted or cited (assessment, not documented statistics). The pattern: the item is the dated, DOI-bearing unit; the container is identified by a yearless serial identifier; container-level DOIs exist as optional conveniences.

## 5. Synthesis — mapped to HVML's choices (analysis, not survey)

Everything below is analysis; the survey facts above are the evidence.

**Option (a): premiere year frozen at mint — `/2016/hughs-vlog`.** This is the option the surveyed systems triangulate toward. Every system that dates a series dates it by its premiere and never re-derives: EIDR's required Series `ReleaseDate` is "the date or year of the first released Episode" with the end of the run as a separate optional field; Wikidata uses `P580` first-air; Wikipedia's year disambiguator is the debut year, frozen in the article title; MusicBrainz derives the release group's date from the earliest release. An ongoing run is uniformly represented as premiere + absent end, so an open-ended series poses no problem for a premiere-year namespace — the year is mint-time metadata about first exhibition, exactly the existing film rule, not a coverage claim.

Revivals fall out cleanly, because every registry that splits, splits at the premiere: Doctor Who classic and revival land at `/1963/…` and `/2005/…` with no collision. The residual collision class is two same-titled series premiering the same year — the same class films already have, handled by the same slug-level disambiguation. One caveat from Wikipedia: its first-resort disambiguator is country, not year (The Office UK/US/AU), so a year path plus native-script slug will still need slug disambiguation when same-titled, same-year series arise across countries; the year alone will not always save the slug.

What the year cannot decide is the prior question of whether a revival is a new series. EIDR/IMDb/TMDb/TheTVDB say Doctor Who 2005 is new; the BBC, Wikipedia, and Wikidata's primary item say it is one continuing series. HVML must pick a policy (the registry-split convention is the defensible one, since all of HVML's equivalence targets except Wikidata's umbrella item split), and the Wikidata bridge-item episode shows the cost of the other choice: one-to-many equivalence links. Sub-item finding worth recording: for a unified-item work, Wikidata's `P2704`/`P345`/`P4983` claims sit on era items, so an HVML record equating to "Doctor Who" must decide which stratum it equates to.

**Option (b): yearless series path.** Precedent exists — ISSN deliberately identifies an ongoing serial with a meaningless, yearless code, and ISAN roots encode no date — but both are opaque identifiers with no collision pressure on a human-readable segment. A yearless slug namespace re-creates the global-uniqueness problem the year path exists to solve; when two same-titled series collide, the disambiguator that every human-readable system (Wikipedia) reaches for is the premiere year, which would then re-enter as slug text (`doctor-who-2005`) — the year returns anyway, minus the structural guarantee. It also splits the registry's grammar into two IRI shapes and forces an irreversible series-vs-film call at mint (EIDR flags mini-series as a gray zone between movie-in-parts and Series; shows commissioned as limited series get renewed).

**Option (c): only episodes/videos get IRIs; series identity lives elsewhere.** This is precisely ISAN's original design — seasons got nothing, the series was a derived 12-digit prefix, not an issued identifier — and ISAN abandoned it: the current schema replaced the serial header with a full Series record and now issues Group (season) ISANs. Crossref shows the same drift: articles-only was the practice, then journal title-level DOIs arrived as encouraged options. EIDR records ISAN serial headers as `Proprietary` alternate IDs (domain `isan.org/serial`) — prefix-derived pseudo-identifiers leak into neighboring systems as second-class citizens. For HVML specifically, every external authority it wants equivalence links to (Wikidata, EIDR, IMDb, TMDb) has a series-level ID; a registry that cannot name the series cannot anchor those links or serve as an annotation target for "this show."

**Seasons.** No surveyed system supports minting seasons as independent, year-keyed, title-derived identifiers, and consumer practice is thin even where season IDs exist: EIDR seasons are full DOIs but mostly carry system-generated names, and EIDR's own distribution guidance routes bundles through Compilation records instead; IMDb has no season IDs at all (per EIDR's footnote); ISAN had none until Groups arrived; Wikidata itemizes seasons at less than a third the rate of series and a seventh the rate of episodes; TMDb and TheTVDB address seasons as ordinal sub-paths of the series (`/tv/{id}/season/{n}`), not as independent citable identifiers (the sub-path form is from general familiarity with those sites, not fetched documentation). The consistent shape: seasons are subordinate, ordinal-addressed grouping records under the series — which suggests HVML, if it names them at all, should address them relative to the series IRI (ordinal suffix), not mint them into the year namespace.

**Net recommendation-shaped conclusion:** option (a), with episodes and any season addressing subordinate to the series IRI, has direct precedent in every surveyed system; (b) has precedent only among opaque identifiers; (c) is the design ISAN and Crossref both migrated away from.
